Walking in the Footsteps of My Ancestors

By tannerr · 14 October 2026

There are certain places in the world that you visit simply because they are famous. You want to see the landmarks, take the pictures, and experience the places you've heard about your entire life. New York City is certainly one of those destinations. Between the towering skyscrapers, crowded streets, historic neighborhoods, and iconic landmarks, there is something almost surreal about finally standing in a city that has appeared in countless movies, photographs, and stories.
But my visit to New York City was about more than seeing the sights. One of the places I was most excited to experience was Ellis Island, a small island in New York Harbor that played an enormous role in American history. For millions of immigrants, Ellis Island was their first introduction to the United States. It represented uncertainty, opportunity, fear, excitement, and the possibility of building an entirely new life.
What made visiting Ellis Island especially meaningful was knowing that my own ancestors had once stood in the same place. They had walked through those halls, waited for their names to be called, and wondered what their futures in America might hold.
There is something incredibly powerful about visiting a historical site and realizing that the people who came before you were actually there. Suddenly, history stops feeling like something you read about in a textbook. It becomes personal.
New York City has a way of making an impression before you even begin exploring. The moment you enter Manhattan, everything seems to move at a different pace. Yellow taxis weave through traffic, pedestrians fill the sidewalks, and buildings rise so high that you sometimes have to tilt your head all the way back just to see their tops.
Growing up hearing about New York, I always imagined it as this enormous, almost overwhelming place. In person, it somehow manages to be exactly that while also feeling completely different from what I expected.
One of the things I found most interesting was how much history exists beneath the modern city. You can walk past glass skyscrapers, luxury stores, and busy restaurants without realizing that the same streets have been traveled by generations of people for hundreds of years.
New York has always been a city of movement. People arrive, people leave, and people come searching for something better. That was true more than a century ago, and in many ways, it is still true today.
Knowing that my ancestors had once arrived in this same city made the experience feel different. I found myself wondering what New York looked like when they first saw it. What did they think of the skyline? Were they excited? Were they terrified? Did they have any idea what their lives would become?
Those questions stayed with me throughout the trip.
One of the most memorable parts of visiting Ellis Island actually happens before you arrive.
The ferry ride through New York Harbor offers incredible views of the Manhattan skyline, the Statue of Liberty, and the surrounding water. As the city slowly gets farther away, you begin to appreciate just how important this harbor has been throughout American history.
For many immigrants traveling to the United States in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, entering New York Harbor was the final stage of an exhausting journey across the Atlantic Ocean.
Some had spent weeks traveling in uncomfortable conditions, carrying only a few belongings and whatever money they had managed to save. Many had left behind their homes, friends, and family members, knowing they might never see them again.
I tried to imagine what that journey must have felt like.
Today, we can board a ferry, take photographs, and enjoy the scenery. We know exactly where we're going, how long the ride will take, and that we'll be returning to Manhattan later in the day.
For immigrants arriving more than a century ago, there were no such guarantees.
They were approaching an unfamiliar country, often without speaking English, and hoping that the sacrifices they had made would lead to a better future.
Seeing the Statue of Liberty from the water was especially meaningful. It is one of those landmarks that everyone recognizes, but viewing it from the harbor gives it an entirely different significance.
For generations of immigrants, the statue symbolized the possibility of freedom and opportunity. It was a sign that they had finally reached the country they had traveled so far to enter.
Standing on the ferry, I couldn't help thinking about how my ancestors might have felt when they first saw it.
When the ferry arrived at Ellis Island, I immediately noticed how peaceful the island felt compared with Manhattan.
The city was still visible across the water, but the noise and constant movement seemed far away. The historic buildings stood quietly against the skyline, surrounded by open space and the harbor.
It was difficult to imagine that this relatively calm place had once processed thousands of immigrants in a single day.
Ellis Island operated as the nation's busiest federal immigration station from 1892 until 1954. During that period, more than 12 million immigrants passed through its facilities. Many Americans today can trace at least part of their family history to people who arrived through the island.
Those numbers are impressive on their own, but they become even more remarkable when you consider that every person represented an individual story.
Each immigrant had a reason for leaving home. Some were escaping poverty or persecution. Others were searching for employment, religious freedom, or opportunities they could not find in their home countries.
They arrived with different languages, traditions, hopes, and fears.
And somehow, all of those stories passed through the same buildings.
Walking toward the main immigration building, I thought about the fact that my ancestors had been among those millions of people.
They were not simply names in an old family record. They were real people who had made a difficult decision to leave everything familiar behind.
And now, generations later, I was standing where they had stood.
The most powerful moment of my visit was entering the Registry Room, commonly known as the Great Hall.
The room is enormous, with high ceilings, large windows, and a distinctive tiled ceiling that stretches above the open floor. Today, visitors can walk through the space and learn about the immigration process through museum exhibits.
But during Ellis Island's busiest years, this room was filled with immigrants waiting to be inspected and questioned by officials.
I remember standing there and looking around, trying to imagine the scene.
Instead of tourists taking photographs, the room would have been crowded with people carrying luggage and documents. Different languages would have echoed through the hall. Families would have waited together, hoping they had everything necessary to enter the country.
Some people probably felt excited. Others were undoubtedly nervous.
For many, the inspection process represented one final obstacle between them and the new lives they hoped to build.
As I stood in the Great Hall, I kept coming back to one thought:
My ancestors may have stood in this exact room.
They may have looked up at the same ceiling. They may have waited in the same space, wondering whether they would be allowed to continue their journey.
There was no way to know precisely where they had stood or what they had been thinking, but the possibility alone made the experience incredibly meaningful.
It is easy to feel disconnected from previous generations. We know our grandparents, and perhaps we have heard stories about our great-grandparents, but eventually family history becomes a collection of names, dates, and places.
Visiting Ellis Island made those distant generations feel much closer.
For a moment, I could imagine myself standing beside them, seeing the room through their eyes.
One of the things I appreciated most about the Ellis Island museum was how it explained the immigration process in a way that went beyond simple dates and statistics.
Arriving immigrants underwent inspections intended to determine whether they met the requirements for admission to the United States. Medical examinations were an important part of the process, and inspectors also asked questions about passengers' backgrounds, destinations, and ability to support themselves.
Although many immigrants were admitted after relatively brief inspections, others faced additional questioning or medical evaluations.
For families who had already endured a long ocean crossing, even a short delay must have been stressful.
The museum helps visitors understand how uncertain the process could feel, particularly for people who did not speak English or who had limited financial resources.
One detail that stood out to me was how much depended on information that seems ordinary today: a person's name, occupation, destination, and family connections.
Imagine traveling thousands of miles and having your future depend on answering questions in an unfamiliar language.
It made me appreciate how much courage it must have taken to immigrate during that time.
I also found myself thinking about the people who did not make it through the process. Although most immigrants arriving at Ellis Island were admitted, some were detained or denied entry.
For those individuals and families, the island could represent heartbreak rather than opportunity.
That complexity is important to remember. Ellis Island is often celebrated as a symbol of the American dream, but the experiences of the people who passed through it were not all the same.
For some, it marked the beginning of a successful new chapter. For others, it was a place of separation, uncertainty, and disappointment.
Understanding both sides made the visit feel more meaningful.
Before visiting Ellis Island, I had always thought of my family's immigration history as something that happened a very long time ago.
I knew that previous generations had come to America, but those stories sometimes felt distant from my everyday life.
Standing inside the immigration building changed that perspective.
I started thinking about all the ordinary decisions my ancestors must have made after arriving.
Where would they live? How would they find work? Who would help them get settled? How would they learn to navigate a new country?
Today, we often think about immigration in terms of major historical events, but for the people experiencing it, life would have been filled with smaller challenges.
Finding a place to sleep, buying food, learning a new language, and adjusting to unfamiliar customs were all part of building a life in America.
I wondered what my ancestors missed most about their original homes.
Was it the food? The language? The people they had left behind?
Did they ever regret leaving? Did they feel proud of the lives they were creating?
There are so many questions I wish I could ask them.
What struck me most was realizing how much of my own life depended on decisions made by people I never had the opportunity to meet.
If they had chosen not to leave their home countries, my family history might have unfolded in an entirely different way.
The places I have lived, the opportunities I have had, and even the people I know are connected, in some way, to the choices those earlier generations made.
That realization gave me a deeper appreciation for my family history.
It also reminded me that the sacrifices people make can have consequences that extend far beyond their own lifetimes.
My ancestors could not have known that, generations later, one of their descendants would return to Ellis Island as a visitor.
Yet there I was, walking through the same building and trying to understand the world they had experienced.
Another meaningful part of visiting Ellis Island was seeing the American Immigrant Wall of Honor.
Located outside near the main immigration building, the wall commemorates immigrants from different periods of American history. Families can have names inscribed to honor their ancestors and preserve their stories.
Walking along the wall, I was struck by the number of names and the different backgrounds they represented.
Every name belonged to someone whose life had been shaped by the experience of immigration.
Some people arrived with relatives. Others came alone. Some were children who had little understanding of why their families were moving. Others were adults making difficult decisions in hopes of creating better opportunities.
Seeing those names reminded me that the history of immigration is not just about the past.
It is about families, identity, and the ways people's stories become connected across generations.
Even when we don't know every detail about our ancestors, we can still appreciate the journeys that brought our families to where they are today.
I spent some time looking out across the harbor from the island.
Manhattan was visible in the distance, its modern skyline standing in sharp contrast to the historic immigration buildings.
The view seemed to capture the relationship between the past and present.
The city had changed dramatically, but Ellis Island remained as a reminder of the people who helped shape it.
After spending time exploring Ellis Island, returning to Manhattan felt different from arriving earlier in the day.
The ferry ride back offered another opportunity to admire the skyline, but now I found myself thinking less about the famous buildings and more about the generations of people who had arrived here before me.
For many immigrants, Manhattan was not simply a place to visit. It was the beginning of an entirely new life.
Some settled in New York City, while others continued traveling to different parts of the United States.
I imagined what it must have been like to step into the crowded streets for the first time, surrounded by unfamiliar sounds, languages, and customs.
Even today, New York can feel overwhelming to someone visiting for the first time.
I can only imagine how intimidating it must have been for someone arriving more than a century ago.
Yet there is something remarkable about the city's ability to bring together people from so many different backgrounds.
Walking through Manhattan after visiting Ellis Island, I noticed how many cultures were represented in the neighborhoods, restaurants, shops, and conversations around me.
It felt like a continuation of the history I had just spent the day learning about.
New York's identity has been shaped by generations of people arriving from around the world, bringing their traditions and experiences with them.
That diversity is one of the things that makes the city so interesting.
Of course, a trip to New York City wouldn't be complete without spending time exploring some of its famous attractions.
Walking through Times Square was almost the complete opposite of the peaceful atmosphere on Ellis Island.
Bright advertisements covered the buildings, crowds filled the sidewalks, and there seemed to be something happening in every direction.
It was loud, busy, and overwhelming in the way that only Times Square can be.
Central Park offered a welcome contrast.
After spending time surrounded by buildings and traffic, walking through the park felt like stepping into a completely different environment.
The open green spaces, winding paths, and views of the surrounding skyline made it easy to understand why the park is such an important part of the city.
I also enjoyed simply walking through different neighborhoods and observing everyday life.
Some of my favorite travel experiences happen when I stop worrying about checking landmarks off a list and instead spend time exploring without a strict plan.
New York is especially good for that.
You can turn a corner and discover a small café, a historic building, an interesting bookstore, or a neighborhood that feels completely different from the one you just left.
The city seems to have endless layers, and every neighborhood has its own personality.
But throughout the trip, I kept thinking about Ellis Island.
Seeing the modern city made me wonder how much New York had changed since my ancestors first arrived.
The skyline would have looked different. Transportation, technology, and daily life would have been almost unrecognizable compared with today.
Yet the city would still have been filled with people searching for opportunities and trying to build lives for themselves.
That connection between past and present became one of my favorite parts of the trip.
